Union, intersection, complement, and difference

Union, intersection, and complement are the three standard Boolean operations on sets.

union: A ∪ Bin A or B, or both
intersection: A ∩ Bin both A and B
complement: Acin Ω, but not in A

Together, these operations can express every finite Boolean combination of sets.

Visualizing the operations

The shaded region shows the result of each fundamental operation.

Union: A ∪ B

A ∪ B = {ω ∈ Ω : ω ∈ A or ω ∈ B}

Union means inclusive “or”: A, or B, or both.

Intersection: A ∩ B

A ∩ B = {ω ∈ Ω : ω ∈ A and ω ∈ B}

Intersection means both conditions hold for the same outcome.

Complement: Ac

Ac = {ω ∈ Ω : ω ∉ A}

A complement is always relative to the chosen sample space Ω.

06

De Morgan’s laws

To negate “or,” every alternative must fail. To negate “and,” at least one condition must fail.

(A ∪ B)c = Ac ∩ Bc

(A ∩ B)c = Ac ∪ Bc

Proof of the first law: choose an arbitrary outcome ω.

ω ∈ (A ∪ B)cstart on the left
⟺ ω ∉ A ∪ Bdefinition of complement
⟺ not(ω ∈ A or ω ∈ B)definition of union
⟺ ω ∉ A and ω ∉ Bordinary logic
⟺ ω ∈ Ac and ω ∈ Bcdefinition of complement
⟺ ω ∈ Ac ∩ Bcdefinition of intersection

The arbitrary outcome belongs to the left set exactly when it belongs to the right set. Therefore the sets are equal.

A general recipe for set proofs

  1. Choose an arbitrary outcome ω from the sample space Ω.
  2. Write what it means for ω to belong to the set on the left.
  3. Translate set membership into logic: union means “or,” intersection means “and,” and complement means “not.”
  4. Simplify the logical statement, then translate it back into set membership on the right.
  5. Because ω was arbitrary, conclude that the relationship holds for every outcome.

To prove equality:   ω ∈ LHS  ⟺  ω ∈ RHS.

To prove inclusion:   assume ω ∈ A, then show ω ∈ B.

07

Disjoint events and partitions

A and B are disjoint  ⟺  A ∩ B = ∅.

Our events “even” and “at least 4” are not disjoint: their overlap is {4, 6}.

A partition cuts Ω into nonempty pieces with no overlaps and no gaps.

{3, 6}
{1, 4}
{2, 5}

C1, …, Ck partition Ω when

Ci ≠ ∅   for every i;

Ci ∩ Cj = ∅   when i ≠ j;

⋃i=1k Ci = Ω.

Exactly one partition event occurs for every outcome.

Partitions will later let us break a hard probability into simpler conditional pieces.

Preview: connecting set operations to probability

Next lecture begins probability. Here is a first look at how set relationships control probability calculations.

Roll a fair six-sided die once and record the number on its upper face.

sample spaceΩ = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
event A: evenA = {2, 4, 6}
event B: at least 4B = {4, 5, 6}

P(Ac) = 1 - P(A)

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)

Why subtract the intersection?

Because adding P(A) and P(B) counts outcomes in the overlap twice.

P(A) = 3/6,   P(B) = 3/6,   P(A ∩ B) = 2/6

P(A ∪ B) = 3/6 + 3/6 - 2/6 = 4/6.

Check directly: A ∪ B = {2, 4, 5, 6}, four of the six equally likely outcomes.

If A ∩ B = ∅, then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
